About 2D1

Familiar Characters is brought to you by 2D1, an independent design practice by Kosuke Futsukaichi. I call it a design house: a place to work with clients and make things of my own.

Everything I do as 2D1 all come from the same habit of caring about the things I surround myself with.

An eye for things

At eight, I had a favorite clothing brand and wanted almost everything I wore to come from there. Whenever my mom took me to the store, I’d pick out clothes, try them on by myself, and carefully fold the ones I didn’t like before passing them under the fitting-room curtain. My little pile of “this ain’t it.” I always cared about how things looked, how they felt, and how they existed in my space.

That interest eventually led me to study design at Simon Fraser University’s School of Interactive Arts & Technology, where I focused on user experience design. The italiaDesign field school widened my view of what design could be. The Italian designers I interviewed spoke of design as an attitude, a way of seeing and living. That felt different from the emphasis on design as a job I’d encountered in North America. It helped me recognize what made something good across different kinds of design, and imagine a practice that could extend beyond my occupation.

Alongside that eye for things, I’d always wanted to make something of my own. As a kid, I pitched ideas for handy inventions to my mom. In my sixth-grade yearbook, I wrote that I wanted to be an “inventor.” After graduating, I joined an early-stage startup as its first employee and founding designer. I liked the co-founders and was excited by the challenge, but I also wanted to learn what it took to build a company in case I wanted to start one someday.

My work took me through other tech startups before I joined Takram Tokyo. There, I’ve worked on digital experiences for larger companies and organizations, alongside designers whose work goes well beyond screens. Watching graphic designers work with print and industrial designers build physical prototypes made designing everyday objects feel more approachable. I began to see how I could explore it too, by learning what I needed to learn and working with people who knew things I didn’t.

Room for more

Even so, when I first imagined 2D1, I planned around what I already knew how to do: a studio taking on client work. It made sense, but it didn’t quite match what I visualized in my daydreams. I wanted room for interests I hadn’t been able to pursue in my work as an employee. I’d always wanted to make everyday objects, but without a background in designing physical things, I kept treating that ambition as a fantasy. Eventually, I had to admit that I was boxing myself in.

I like client work. I have huge respect for people starting their own thing, and I enjoy helping them make something they believe in. But I also want to follow my own ideas, including the small, persistent ones—like wanting to design a shoehorn. A design house gave me room for both, and for the objects I keep meeting along the way.

Familiar Characters

I tend to see objects as companions. They live in our homes, join our routines, and become familiar in their own way. A well-designed object with a clear point of view can feel like it has a soul. What draws me in is how that seemingly ordinary thing can come to matter so much to someone. We have a mug we reach for first, a pen that feels right in our hand, a shoehorn we use before heading out the door. Something caught our attention at first: a proportion, a color, a detail that made us smile. But we keep reaching for it because it works well and feels right in our lives. With use, it gathers marks, habits, and memories.

What starts as an object with character becomes a character in our lives. That’s what I mean by Familiar Characters. Objects become meaningful because life happens around them, and that’s what I hope the things I make will have the chance to do.

Reduction with wit

That perspective shapes how I design, whether I’m working on an object or with a client on a brand or digital product. I refer to my approach as reduction with wit: finding the idea at the core, removing what gets in its way, and keeping the detail that gives it character. An interface needs to make it clear what to do next, while expressing the character of the brand through its words, visuals, and behavior. An identity needs to give a company a recognizable face. In both, I’m looking for something that works well and feels like itself.

In the studio, that starts with understanding what you’re trying to do and what’s getting in the way. I work with founders and small businesses to turn that understanding into a visual identity, a website, or a digital product. We define the work together, and I can stay with it from the first idea through to the finished design, or focus on the part where you need me.

Things of my own, and things worth sharing

The shop lets me follow the same questions on my own terms. Sometimes I see an everyday object and wish the shape, material, or way it works were a little different. I start wondering what my interpretation would be, and then I want to make it. I’m starting with 2D Tote, a canvas tote—an ordinary thing with plenty of decisions to care about, from the way it opens to what’s inside.

Looking closely at what others make is part of that process too. In the Familiar Characters collection, I hand-pick everyday objects with a clear point of view and a little wit. I personally wish I owned everything there. Sharing them is another way to talk about what I value in design, through the things themselves.

That’s the house I’m building: a place where paying attention can lead to making something, for a client or for everyday life. If you have something in mind that you’d like to make together, I’d love to hear about it.
